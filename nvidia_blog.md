Less Data, Better ReID: Researchers Improve Clothing-Change Person Re-Identification with NVIDIA TAO

Identifying the same person across multiple cameras is a fundamental challenge in computer vision. A person may appear at a different scale, under different lighting, from an unfamiliar angle, or in clothing that no longer resembles their previous appearance.

These variations make person re-identification, or ReID, particularly difficult in real-world multi-camera environments. Building models that can handle these conditions often requires large collections of annotated person images captured across many cameras—a process that is expensive, time-consuming, and difficult to scale.

Researchers from \[UNIVERSITY / RESEARCH GROUP\], supported by the NVIDIA Academic Grant Program, investigated whether carefully selected synthetic data could reduce this dependency while improving ReID performance under challenging clothing-change and domain-shift conditions.

Their experiments produced a counterintuitive result: using more synthetic images did not necessarily produce a better model. Instead, removing repetitive synthetic samples enabled the team to train a more data-efficient ReID model that achieved a 20 percentage-point improvement in mean Average Precision on the LTCC benchmark.

Finding the Same Person Across Changing Conditions

A ReID model receives cropped images of people and converts each image into an embedding—a numerical representation of the person’s visual characteristics. Embeddings produced from images of the same person should be located close to one another, while embeddings belonging to different people should remain separated.

This allows a system to take one query image and retrieve the most likely matches from a gallery containing observations from other cameras.

However, many ReID models learn heavily from clothing color, texture, and appearance. When a person changes clothing, some of the strongest visual cues disappear. Differences between camera environments can further reduce performance.

When More Synthetic Data Becomes Repetitive Data

Synthetic data offers a scalable way to introduce new identities, camera views, poses, and appearance variations without collecting every example from real-world environments.

The team initially created a synthetic training directory containing 233,840 person crops across 39 synthetic identities. However, an audit of the dataset revealed that many images were near-duplicate variations of the same person at the same moment.

Some person-and-moment groups contained as many as 150 visual variants. Although these images appeared slightly different, they did not necessarily provide 150 independent learning examples.

To reduce this repetition, the researchers grouped images according to:

\- Person identity  
\- Camera  
\- Sequence  
\- Source frame  
\- Original person bounding box

They then retained a maximum of three variants from each underlying person-and-moment group.

This filtering process reduced the synthetic dataset from 233,840 images to 6,152 images, removing 97.37% of the crop volume while preserving all 39 synthetic identities.

Rather than repeatedly showing the model minor variations of the same visual moment, the filtered dataset emphasized diversity across identities, cameras, sequences, and observations.

Training ReID Models with NVIDIA TAO

The researchers used NVIDIA TAO Toolkit to train and evaluate ReID models based on a Swin Transformer backbone.

The experiments were executed using the NVIDIA TAO Toolkit 6.0 PyTorch container from the NVIDIA NGC catalog. Containerized execution helped the team maintain a consistent training environment across experiments and isolate workloads across available GPUs.

The research pipeline included:

\- Swin Transformer-based ReID training  
\- Market-1501-compatible dataset organization  
\- Stable checkpoint creation and evaluation  
\- Mean Average Precision and ranked retrieval metrics  
\- Separate evaluations for each target dataset  
\- Query-and-gallery match visualization  
\- Multi-domain training and stress evaluation

Training and checkpoint evaluation workloads were distributed between NVIDIA GPUs. While one GPU trained the generalized multi-domain model, another processed checkpoint validation queues, enabling the team to evaluate model development across many training stages.

Less Synthetic Data, Stronger LTCC Performance

The largest improvement appeared on LTCC, a challenging ReID benchmark that includes clothing changes.

The Swin model trained using only the real LTCC training set reached:

\- 23.8% mAP  
\- 50.3% Rank-1 accuracy

After augmenting the training set with 6,152 filtered synthetic crops, the best checkpoint reached:

\- 43.8% mAP  
\- 76.1% Rank-1 accuracy

This represents an improvement of:

\- 20.0 percentage points in mAP  
\- 25.8 percentage points in Rank-1 accuracy

The filtered synthetic experiment also slightly outperformed the older unfiltered synthetic-data experiment, which reached 43.1% mAP and 74.4% Rank-1 accuracy, despite using a much larger and more repetitive synthetic pool.

The result suggests that synthetic-data composition can be more important than raw synthetic-data volume—particularly when the target dataset contains challenging appearance changes.

Synthetic Augmentation Does Not Help Every Domain Equally

The team also tested the same filtered synthetic subset on Duke and PRCC.

On PRCC, the filtered synthetic model achieved 72.4% mAP, compared with 72.1% mAP for the real-data-only Swin model. Both configurations reached 98.6% Rank-1 accuracy.

On Duke, the filtered synthetic model reached 86.4% mAP and 89.7% Rank-1 accuracy. A separately evaluated plain Duke model remained stronger, reaching 89.0% mAP and 90.6% Rank-1 accuracy.

These results show that synthetic augmentation is not universally beneficial. The largest improvement appeared in the clothing-change domain represented by LTCC, while the effect was limited on PRCC and did not surpass the strongest plain model on Duke.

This observation helped the researchers refine their central conclusion: synthetic data should be selected according to the variation missing from the target domain rather than added indiscriminately.

Building a Multi-Domain ReID Model

Beyond dataset-specific models, the team trained a generalized Swin model using real training identities from Duke, LTCC, and PRCC together with a controlled selection of synthetic data.

To avoid collisions between datasets, person and camera identifiers were assigned separate namespaces. Real validation identities were held out before training, and the official query-and-gallery folders of each benchmark remained separate for final evaluation.

The generalized training set contained:

\- 154,258 training images  
\- 875 identities  
\- Real samples from three ReID domains  
\- A deterministic selection from the synthetic pool

At its final checkpoint, the generalized model achieved raw retrieval results of:

\- 55.4% mAP on Duke  
\- 29.5% mAP on LTCC  
\- 71.5% mAP on PRCC  
\- 46.2% mAP on a combined cross-domain stress split

The generalized model was not intended to replace every dataset-specific model. Instead, it explored the tradeoff between peak domain-specific accuracy and broader cross-domain applicability.

Making ReID Experiments Interpretable

To make the experiment results easier to inspect, the researchers developed an interactive ReID dashboard.

The dashboard enables users to:

\- Select a target dataset  
\- Compare checkpoint performance  
\- Inspect mAP and ranked retrieval curves  
\- Upload or select a query person image  
\- View the highest-ranked gallery matches  
\- Identify correct matches and failure cases  
\- Compare dataset-specific and generalized models

This visualization layer helps bridge the gap between aggregate metrics and real model behavior. Two checkpoints with similar mAP values may still fail in different ways, and retrieval examples can reveal whether the model is relying on clothing color, body shape, background information, or other visual cues.

Toward More Data-Efficient ReID Deployment

The findings show that synthetic data can substantially improve person re-identification under clothing changes—but only when the synthetic samples provide meaningful diversity.

The team’s filtering method reduced synthetic-data volume by more than 97% while producing the strongest LTCC result in the experiment repository. This reduced storage and training-data management requirements and provided evidence that repeatedly generated variations of the same observation can offer diminishing value.

Future work will focus on:

\- Testing the filtering policy with additional ReID datasets  
\- Measuring inference latency and throughput in multi-camera environments  
\- Evaluating TensorRT-optimized deployment  
\- Developing domain-aware synthetic sample selection  
\- Investigating failure cases under severe occlusion and viewpoint changes  
\- Comparing transformer and convolutional ReID backbones  
\- Extending dashboard support to live camera streams

The team also plans to evaluate responsible deployment practices. Person re-identification systems can involve sensitive data, making access control, retention policies, human oversight, and compliance with applicable privacy regulations essential parts of any real-world implementation.

Supported by the NVIDIA Academic Grant Program, the project demonstrates a practical research direction for developing more data-efficient and interpretable ReID systems using NVIDIA accelerated computing and NVIDIA TAO.
